## The words she'll bring home

Some of these are new names for things you already know. Some are new ideas. This tells you which is which.

**Regroup**
Ten ones swapped for one ten, or ten tens for one hundred, when a column adds up to ten or more.
*You may have called it:* carrying.
→ Addition

**Exchange**
The swap in the other direction — breaking one hundred into ten tens so there's enough to subtract from.
*You may have called it:* borrowing.
→ Subtraction

**Ones, not units**
The digit in the ones column. We say *ones* because it names the value and sits in a sequence — ones, tens, hundreds — in a way *units* doesn't.
→ Place value and exchange

**Partition**
Splitting a number into parts, usually by place value: 23 becomes 20 and 3. Almost every mental method starts here.
→ From mental to written

**Number bond**
A pair of numbers that make a particular total — usually 10, 20 or 100. Known instantly, and known as a relationship rather than a recited fact.
→ Part–whole and number bonds

**Part–whole**
A diagram showing a number and the parts it's made of. Six at the top, four and two underneath.
→ Part–whole and number bonds

**Bridging through ten**
Getting to ten first, then carrying on: 8 + 5 becomes 8 + 2 + 3. Ten is the number our whole system is built on, so it's nearly always the easiest route.
→ The number line

**Difference**
How far apart two numbers are. One of the three meanings of subtraction, and often found by counting on rather than taking away — which is why 402 − 398 is a small hop, not a hard calculation.
→ Subtraction

**Compensating**
Changing a calculation into an easier one and correcting afterwards. 543 − 199 becomes 543 − 200, then give one back.
→ Subtraction

**Adjusting**
Shifting both numbers by the same amount, which leaves the difference between them unchanged. 402 − 398 becomes 404 − 400.
→ Subtraction

**Inverse**
Addition and subtraction undo each other, and so do multiplication and division. Used for checking: if 47 + 36 = 83, then 83 − 36 should give 47 back.
→ Estimation and choosing a method

**Array**
Objects arranged in rows and columns. Four rows of three shows at a glance why 3 × 4 and 4 × 3 give the same answer.
→ Multiplication

**Grouping and sharing**
The two meanings of division. Sharing is twelve sweets between three children. Grouping is how many threes there are in twelve. Formal written division depends on grouping, which is why we teach both.
→ Division

**Chunking**
Dividing by repeatedly taking away groups you already know: 96 take away sixty (that's ten sixes), then take away thirty-six (that's six more). The stage between grouping and short division.
→ Division

**Bar model**
A drawing that shows the structure of a problem — what's the whole, what are the parts, what's missing — before any calculating starts.
→ The bar model

**Base 10 blocks and place-value counters**
The two kinds of equipment you'll see. Blocks show how big a number is; counters show what place it's in.
→ Place value and exchange

**Estimate**
A rough answer worked out *before* the real one, so she can tell whether her answer is sensible. We ask for it first rather than as a check at the end.
→ Estimation and choosing a method

**Efficient**
Choosing the quickest sensible method rather than always reaching for the written one. Deciding that 402 − 398 doesn't need column subtraction is being efficient.
→ Estimation and choosing a method

**Fluency**
Knowing facts and methods well enough to use them without thinking hard, so that attention is free for the difficult part. Not the same as being fast.
→ What actually helps at home

## Before you start

This guide is about **methods** — the ways of working your daughter will meet, why we teach them the way we do, and how they differ from the way you were probably taught.

It is not a checklist. Each method has a note saying roughly when you'll see it, because parents reasonably want to know, but children arrive at fluency at different points and in different orders. A child still using an expanded written method a year after it was introduced is not behind. She is using the version that still makes sense to her, which is exactly what we want.

If you read only one section, make it the next one.

---

## The idea that runs through everything

Every written method your daughter learns is built in four stages:

**work it out in your head → jot down what you did → write it out in full → write it out efficiently**

Parents almost only ever see the two ends of that chain. You see a child working something out in her head in Year 2, and you see a compact column method in Year 5, and the two stages in between can look like a detour.

They aren't. Each stage is the previous one recorded more efficiently. Nothing new is introduced at any point — the reasoning in the compact method is the same reasoning she used in her head, just written in fewer marks.

This is why we don't jump straight to the efficient version. A child given short multiplication before she has partitioned 23 into 20 and 3 has no way of understanding why the digits go where they go, and nothing to fall back on when she misremembers. A child who has been through the chain can rebuild the method from what she already knows.

### Working it out, and writing it down

Written methods are not a replacement for working it out. They are what we reach for when the numbers get too big to hold in your head, and a child who can do a calculation mentally and chooses to should be encouraged — that judgement is a large part of what we're building.

Working mentally is not the same as working silently, though. We still ask children to explain how they got there, and to record enough that someone else could follow it. Partly that's a habit worth forming early, while the numbers are still small enough that writing anything down feels unnecessary — because by the time it stops feeling unnecessary, it's too late to start. Mostly it's practical: a child who has written down her steps can find where she went wrong, and can pick a calculation back up when it has more parts than she can hold at once.

There's a difference here worth naming. "I just knew it" is exactly the answer we want for 6 × 7 — that's a fact, and instant recall is the goal. For anything with steps in it, we want her to be able to say what the steps were.

You'll see a lot of equipment in your daughter's books — cubes, blocks, counters, strips. It's worth saying plainly that this is not a beginner's version of maths that children grow out of. Apparatus is how a new idea is made visible, and it stays available all the way up the school. A Year 5 child meeting decimals for the first time may well reach for place-value counters, and that isn't a step backwards. It's the same move a mathematician makes when she draws a diagram.

# The methods

## 1. Part–whole and number bonds

**What it is**

A number shown as being made of other numbers. Six is four and two. Six is five and one. Six is three and three.

**Why we use it**

Before a child can add, she needs to know that numbers are made of other numbers. Number bonds — the pairs that make ten, then twenty, then a hundred — are the facts she'll use for the rest of her mathematical life, and they are far more useful known as relationships than recited as a list.

A child who knows that 6 and 4 make 10 also knows that 10 take away 6 is 4, and that 60 and 40 make 100, and that 0.6 and 0.4 make 1. That's one piece of knowledge doing four jobs. A child who has only memorised "six plus four equals ten" has one.

**How you may have been taught it**

Probably as a list to learn by heart, tested by speed. We still want the facts known instantly — but we build them from the relationship first, so that when a child forgets one she can rebuild it rather than being stuck.

**When you'll see it**

From Reception, and it never really stops. Bonds within 10 in Year 1, within 20 and to 100 in Year 2, and the same relationships reappearing with decimals in Years 4 and 5.

---

## 2. The number line, and bridging through 10

**What it is**

**Why we use it**

Ten is the number our whole system is built on, so getting to ten is nearly always the easiest route. To work out 8 + 5, a child splits the 5 into 2 and 3: 8 and 2 makes 10, then 3 more makes 13. That's bridging.

It looks slower than just knowing the answer, and for a while it is. What it buys is a method that keeps working — the same move handles 68 + 5, and 6.8 + 0.5, and 68,000 + 5,000. A child who has only memorised her addition facts has to start again each time the numbers change shape.

The number line is where she sees it. It's a picture of the journey, not a calculation to be completed.

**How you may have been taught it**

Many parents were taught to count on with fingers and told, gently or otherwise, that they shouldn't. We're relaxed about fingers: they're a perfectly good way of keeping track while a fact is still being learned. What we want is for the counting to become unnecessary because the relationship is known, not because it's been forbidden.

**When you'll see it**

Bridging through 10 is a Year 2 focus, but the number line as a way of thinking runs all the way through — you'll see it again for negative numbers, decimals, rounding and fractions.

---

## 3. Place value and exchange

**What it is**

Every written method rests on one idea: ten of anything can be exchanged for one of the next thing up. Ten ones make a ten. Ten tens make a hundred. And in reverse: one hundred can be exchanged back into ten tens whenever you need them.

**Why we use it**

Nearly every mistake in written calculation is an exchange going wrong, not an addition or subtraction going wrong. A child who can confidently exchange 4,003 into 3 thousands, 9 hundreds, 9 tens and 13 ones can do any column subtraction you give her. A child who can't will get stuck on the zeros however many times she practises the steps.

You'll see two kinds of equipment here and both stay available throughout the school. **Base 10 blocks** are proportional — a hundred square really is a hundred times the size of a ones cube — which is what makes them right for building the idea in the first place. **Place-value counters** aren't: a counter marked 100 is the same size as one marked 1, and only the label tells you its value. That sounds like a step backwards and is actually a step forwards, because she's now holding the value in her head rather than reading it off the object. It also means she can model 2,476 on a desk, which she can't do with a thousand cube for every thousand. You'll see counters used more from around Year 4, when the mathematics is about lining up columns and exchanging between them rather than about how big a hundred is.

So we spend longer on exchange than parents often expect, and we keep coming back to it.

**How you may have been taught it**

You may have learned "units" rather than "ones". The change is deliberate: *ones* names the value, and it sits in a sequence — ones, tens, hundreds — in a way *units* doesn't. It also avoids the confusion when *unit* later means something else entirely, as in unit fractions.

**When you'll see it**

From Year 1 with tens and ones, extending a place each year, and reappearing in Year 4 and 5 as the decimal places open up to the right.

## 4. From mental to written

**What it is**

| Stage | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| In her head | "Twenty fours are eighty, three fours are twelve, so ninety-two." |
| Jotted down | 20 × 4 = 80, 3 × 4 = 12, 80 + 12 = 92 |
| Written out in full | The area model — one rectangle split into a part worth 80 and a part worth 12 |
| Written efficiently | Short multiplication |

**Why we use it**

This is the chain described at the start of the guide, made concrete. Each panel is the one before it, recorded more briefly.

The jotting stage is the one parents ask about most, because it looks like extra work. It isn't. She's holding part of the calculation on paper so that her head is free for the rest of it. That is what mathematicians do, and it's what anyone does when they work out a restaurant bill on a napkin.

**How you may have been taught it**

Most likely straight to the last panel, learned as a sequence of steps to be repeated accurately. That works well for children who are already fluent and fails badly for children who aren't, because there's nothing underneath it to fall back on.

If you were taught that way and it worked for you, your daughter will still recognise your method — it's the fourth panel. The three before it are the explanation you may never have been given.

**When you'll see it**

The chain repeats for every operation at different ages. Broadly: the mental stage from Year 1 or 2, jottings from Year 2 or 3, the full written method a year later, the efficient version a year after that.

## 5. Addition

**What it is**

**Why we use it**

Children meet the expanded layout first because it shows what is actually happening: the ones are added, the tens are added, and where a column makes ten or more, that ten is regrouped into the next column. The compact method records the same thinking more quickly.

We teach them in that order so that when a child makes a mistake in the compact method she has something to fall back on that still makes sense to her, rather than a rule she can only half-remember.

Two mental strategies stay in play alongside the written method, and they're worth knowing about because they look like cheating and aren't. **Compensating**: 199 + 46 is awkward, so add 200 and give one back — 246 − 1 = 245. **Adjusting**: 68 + 7 can be shifted to 70 + 5. In both cases she has changed the calculation into an easier one and corrected for the change. Both are more efficient than the column method here, and choosing them is a good sign.

Alongside all of it we keep asking for an estimate first. A child who says "it'll be about four thousand" before she starts has a way of knowing whether her answer is sensible.

**How you may have been taught it**

Probably the compact method straight away, and possibly with the language of "carrying" — writing a small 1 underneath and saying "carry the one". We say *regroup* instead: ten ones have been regrouped as one ten, and that small 1 is the ten arriving in the next column. It's the same mark on the page. The difference is that "regroup" says what happened and "carry" doesn't.

If you show your daughter the way you were taught, you won't confuse her — the layout is the same. It's worth using her words for it, though.

**When you'll see it**

Expanded column addition in Year 3 with three-digit numbers. Compact column addition with four digits in Year 4. By Year 5 we'd expect it to be quick and reliable, and extending to decimals.

## 6. Subtraction

**What it is**

**Why we use it**

The most important thing to say about subtraction is that the written method is often not the best choice.

Take 402 − 398. A child who reaches for column subtraction faces two exchanges across a zero and will very likely get it wrong. A child who notices the two numbers are close and counts on from 398 has the answer in a second, and knows it's right. Both children can subtract. One of them has chosen well.

In class we ask "what's the most efficient strategy here?" — and the answer is genuinely sometimes the column method and sometimes not. Learning to tell the difference is the skill.

Two strategies you may not have been taught are worth knowing about:

**Compensating** — 543 − 199 is awkward, so subtract 200 and give one back: 343 + 1 = 344.

**Adjusting both numbers** — 402 − 398 can be shifted to 404 − 400, which is 4. Adding 2 to both numbers leaves the difference between them untouched. This is also *why* counting on works, and once a child sees that, subtraction across zeros stops being frightening.

Both rest on subtraction meaning *difference*, not just *take away*. That's why we teach three meanings from Year 1 rather than one — taking away, finding a missing part, and finding the difference. A child who only knows take away has no way of seeing that 402 − 398 is a small hop.

When the numbers do warrant a written method, it depends on exchange: one thousand exchanged for ten hundreds, one hundred for ten tens. We use place-value counters so that a child watches the exchange happen before she records it.

**How you may have been taught it**

Two possibilities. Many parents were taught "borrowing" — cross out the digit, borrow one from next door, pay it back at the bottom. Others met equal addition, adding ten to the top and one to the bottom row. We say *exchange*, which is closest to borrowing but without the paying-back step, because nothing is ever borrowed or repaid: the number is simply written differently.

There's a third difference worth naming. Many parents were taught that subtraction always means take away. Your daughter will use *difference* too, and will sometimes solve a subtraction by adding. That isn't a workaround — it's the same operation seen from the other end.

If she's stuck on a subtraction with zeros, the most useful question isn't "what do you do next" but "can you show me 4,003 in counters" — because the difficulty is nearly always in the exchange, not in the subtracting.

**When you'll see it**

Counting on to find a difference from Year 1. Bridging through 10 in Year 2. Exchange in three-digit subtraction in Year 3, four digits including zeros in Year 4. From Year 5 the emphasis shifts to choosing — and choosing a mental strategy where one will do is a mark of confidence, not a shortcut.

## 7. Multiplication

**What it is**

**Why we use it**

The grid and the written method are the same calculation. The rectangle is split into a part worth 20 × 4 and a part worth 3 × 4; the written method lists those two parts and adds them.

Seeing them side by side is the point. It's what stops the written method looking like a new set of rules and makes it look like a way of recording something she has already done. When she moves on to short multiplication, those two parts are still there — they've just been folded into one line.

Underneath it all is one idea, called distributivity: you can break a number up, multiply the pieces, and add the results. It's why 23 × 4 can become 20 × 4 plus 3 × 4, and it's the same idea that makes algebra work later.

**How you may have been taught it**

Many parents met the grid method in school and some still prefer it — it's a perfectly good method and it's the same mathematics as the area model, drawn slightly differently. Others went straight to the column layout.

If you learned times tables by chanting, that's still worth doing. Instant recall of the facts is what frees a child up to think about the harder part. We just want her to know what the facts mean as well as what they are.

**When you'll see it**

Arrays and equal groups from Year 1. Tables facts and the idea that 3 × 4 and 4 × 3 give the same answer in Year 2. Partitioning to multiply two-digit numbers in Year 3. Short multiplication in Year 4.

---

## 8. Long multiplication

**What it is**

**Why we use it**

Exactly the same idea as the last entry, with more places. 3,247 × 26 is 3,247 × 20 plus 3,247 × 6. The two rows of the written method are those two products, and the final answer adds them.

**How you may have been taught it**

Almost certainly this way — and quite possibly with no explanation of the zero at the end of the second row. That zero is there because the row is multiplying by 20, not by 2. Being told to "put a zero down" and not why is one of the most common gaps parents tell us about in their own schooling.

If your daughter can explain that zero, she has understood the method. If she can only remember to write it, she hasn't yet.

**When you'll see it**

Year 5 and 6, once short multiplication is secure.

## 9. Division

**What it is**

| Stage | 96 ÷ 6 |
|---|---|
| In her head | "How many sixes in 96? Ten sixes is 60, that leaves 36, that's six more sixes. Sixteen." |
| Jotted down (chunking) | 96 − 60 (that's 10 sixes), 36 − 36 (that's 6 sixes), answer 16 |
| Written out in full | 96 partitioned into 60 and 36 |
| Written efficiently | Short division |

**Why we use it**

Division has two meanings and children need both. **Sharing** — twelve sweets between three children — is the one they meet first and find natural. **Grouping** — how many threes are there in twelve — is the one that formal division actually depends on, and it's the one that gets skipped.

Chunking is grouping written down. A child taking away ten sixes at a time is doing exactly what short division does in compressed form. When she later writes "how many sixes in nine, one remainder three", that's the same question at a different scale.

This is also why we avoid calling it the bus stop method. The layout does look like a bus stop, and children will call it that among themselves, but naming a method after its shape suggests it's a trick to be remembered rather than something that follows from what she already knows.

**How you may have been taught it**

Very likely the same short division layout, learned as a sequence of steps. If you have an older child you may have seen more chunking in their books than you'll see in your daughter's — it's the second stage above, and we now move on from it more quickly.

The phrase that helps most at home is "how many sixes are in that" rather than "six goes into that". The first describes the maths. The second is a form of words.

**When you'll see it**

Sharing and grouping from Reception. Partitioning to divide in Year 3. Short division with exchange in Year 4. Remainders in Year 5 — and deciding what a remainder *means* in a real problem is genuinely harder than calculating it.

## 10. Long division

**What it is**

**Why we use it**

The same reasoning again, with a two-digit divisor. How many 24s in 43? One, with 19 left over. Exchange that 19 into the next column and carry on.

Long division has a reputation as the hardest thing in primary maths, and it is the point at which a child who has been remembering steps rather than understanding them tends to come unstuck. A child who is confident with grouping and with exchange finds it demanding but sensible.

**How you may have been taught it**

Possibly this way, possibly by chunking, possibly not at all — long division moved in and out of the curriculum over the years. If you find yourself unable to help with it, you're in a very large group, and saying "I don't remember this one, show me how you've been taught it" is a genuinely good thing for your daughter to hear.

**When you'll see it**

Year 6.

## 11. Fractions as numbers

**What it is**

**Why we use it**

Most adults were taught fractions as parts of a shape — half a pizza, a quarter of a cake. That's a fine starting point and it isn't enough, because it doesn't tell you where three quarters *is*.

A fraction is a number. It has a position on the number line, it sits between other numbers, and you can count in fractions the way you count in ones. A child who can find ¾ on a number line, and can say that it's bigger than ⅔ and smaller than 1, understands something a child who can only shade three parts of four does not.

That's why you'll see fractions on number lines as often as in circles and rectangles.

**How you may have been taught it**

Shapes, almost certainly, followed at some point by rules that arrived without explanation — turn it upside down and multiply, find a common denominator. Your daughter will meet those rules too, but later, and after she has seen why they work.

**When you'll see it**

Halves and quarters of shapes and quantities from Year 1. Fractions on a number line from Year 3. Equivalence, comparing and ordering through Years 4 and 5. Calculating with fractions in Years 5 and 6.

## 12. The bar model

**What it is**

**Why we use it**

The bar model is not a way of calculating. It's a way of seeing what a problem is asking before any calculating starts.

Most word-problem mistakes aren't arithmetic mistakes. A child adds when she should have subtracted, or divides the wrong way round. The bar model shows the structure — what's the whole, what are the parts, what's known, what's missing — and once that's visible the operation is usually obvious.

It is probably the single most useful thing in this guide to try yourself. If you have five minutes, [open the Bar Model Builder](Interactive_Maths_Tools_v40_1/Bar_Model_Builder_v3.html) and put a problem into it. It explains itself faster than any paragraph will.

**How you may have been taught it**

You almost certainly weren't. This is genuinely new to most parents. It came into English schools from Singapore and it has done more to improve children's problem-solving than any other single change in recent years.

**When you'll see it**

From Year 1, in a simple form, and increasingly through the school. By Year 6 it's handling ratio, percentage and multi-step problems.

## 13. Estimation and choosing a method

**What it is**

Not a method — a habit. Before calculating: roughly what should this be? After calculating: is that sensible?

There's no wrong method on this page — every route shown gets the right answer. The question is which one is least work for these particular numbers, and that changes from question to question.

### Addition

**199 + 46**
- *Compensate:* 200 + 46 = 246, then give one back → **245**
- *Column method:* works, four steps, one regroup
- **Quickest here:** compensating. The 199 is one away from a friendly number, and spotting that is the whole trick.

**3,500 + 2,500**
- *Known bonds:* 35 + 25 = 60, so 3,500 + 2,500 = **6,000**
- *Column method:* works, three regroups
- **Quickest here:** the bond. Recognising 35 and 25 inside the bigger numbers is what makes it instant.

**2,476 + 1,589**
- *Column method:* **4,065**
- *Mental route:* there isn't a neat one — the numbers aren't near anything friendly
- **Quickest here:** the written method. Not every calculation has a shortcut, and this is what written methods are for.

**18.5 + 6.75**
- *Column method, aligning the points:* **25.25**
- *Partition:* 18 + 6 = 24, 0.5 + 0.75 = 1.25, total 25.25
- **Quickest here:** either. Worth showing two legitimate routes to the same answer.

### Subtraction

**402 − 398**
- *Count on from 398:* two to 400, two more to 402 → **4**
- *Adjust both:* 404 − 400 → **4**
- *Column method:* two exchanges across a zero, and easy to get wrong
- **Quickest here:** counting on. The numbers are close, so the difference is small — no need to take anything away.

**543 − 199**
- *Compensate:* 543 − 200 = 343, give one back → **344**
- *Column method:* works, two exchanges
- **Quickest here:** compensating.

**4,003 − 1,786**
- *Column method:* **2,217**
- *Mental route:* not realistically
- **Quickest here:** the written method — and this is exactly the calculation the exchange work in Year 4 is preparing for.

**8,000 − 2,750**
- *Count on:* 2,750 to 3,000 is 250; 3,000 to 8,000 is 5,000 → **5,250**
- *Column method:* works, three exchanges across zeros
- **Quickest here:** counting on, if she's confident. The written method is a perfectly reasonable choice too.

### Multiplication

**23 × 4**
- *Partition:* 20 × 4 = 80, 3 × 4 = 12 → **92**
- *Short multiplication:* same answer, written down
- **Quickest here:** in her head. The written method is there for when the numbers get bigger.

**6 × 99**
- *Compensate:* 6 × 100 = 600, take off 6 → **594**
- *Long multiplication:* works, more steps
- **Quickest here:** compensating. Same move as 199 + 46, one operation along.

**35 × 20**
- *Factorise:* 35 × 2 = 70, then × 10 → **700**
- *Long multiplication:* works
- **Quickest here:** the double-then-times-ten route.

**3,247 × 26**
- *Long multiplication:* **84,422**
- *Mental route:* no
- **Quickest here:** the written method.

### Division

**96 ÷ 6**
- *Partition:* 60 ÷ 6 = 10, 36 ÷ 6 = 6 → **16**
- *Chunking:* take away ten sixes, then six sixes → 16
- *Short division:* same answer
- **Quickest here:** partitioning — but all three are the same reasoning at different levels of detail, which is the point of this one.

**150 ÷ 25**
- *Count in 25s:* four 25s make 100, two more make 150 → **6**
- *Short division:* works
- **Quickest here:** counting in 25s. Knowing the 25s pays off here and with money and time.

**936 ÷ 6**
- *Partition:* 900 ÷ 6 = 150, 36 ÷ 6 = 6 → **156**
- *Short division:* **156**
- **Quickest here:** genuinely either. Worth including precisely because the answer isn't obvious.

**4,368 ÷ 24**
- *Long division:* **182**
- *Mental route:* no
- **Quickest here:** the written method.


**Why we use it**

By Years 5 and 6, choosing the method *is* the skill. The child who reaches for column subtraction every time is not the strongest mathematician in the room; the one who looks at the numbers first and decides is.

Estimating is what makes that possible. A child who has said "about 4,000" before she starts will notice when her answer comes out as 406. A child who hasn't will write down whatever the procedure gave her.

This is also the answer to a question parents often ask — whether it matters that a child gets the right answer by an inefficient route. In the short term, no. Over time, yes: efficiency is a form of understanding, and a child who always uses the heaviest available method is telling us she doesn't yet trust the lighter ones.

**How you may have been taught it**

Often not at all, or as "checking your answer" tacked on at the end and rarely done. We put it at the beginning instead, where it changes what happens next.

**When you'll see it**

Throughout, with real weight from Year 4 onwards.

## 14. What actually helps at home

The most useful thing you can do is talk. Not drill — talk.

Ask her how she worked it out, and mean it. Ask whether there was another way. When she gets one wrong, ask what she was thinking rather than showing her the right answer; the thinking is where the fix is. Estimate out loud in the car. Notice when maths is doing something useful — halving a recipe, working out change, reading a timetable.

What helps less than parents expect is pushing on to the next method before the current one is secure. A child who can perform column multiplication but can't say why the second row starts with a zero has learned something fragile. She'll be faster this term and stuck next year. Speed and fluency look similar from the outside and are not the same thing.

And if she's finding something hard, the most useful question to bring to us is "what is she thinking that isn't right yet" rather than "what should she practise". We can usually tell you, and the answer is nearly always more specific — and more fixable — than more practice.
